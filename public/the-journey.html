The story behind the names

DIRT DON’T LIE

THE JOURNEY OF LOOPY CARTER & DA HOODFARMER

DMV Roots. Southern Soul. Farmer’s Heart. Entrepreneur’s Mind.

This is not a fictional origin story. It is a real journey shaped by family, discipline, adversity, agriculture, and the decision to keep building when the road gets difficult.

1. The roots: a family story bigger than one name

Loopy Carter’s story is connected to the Carter family of Northern Virginia, whose history reaches back to Keziah Hatton Carter’s 1842 purchase of land that became known as Cartersville and Freedom Hill. Raised within the family and its traditions, Loopy carries a living connection to a history of land, farming, community, and Black and Indigenous family heritage. That legacy is not a decoration for a biography. It is part of the ground beneath the story.

His roots in the Washington, D.C.–Maryland–Northern Virginia region travel with him into his life in South Carolina. The journey is both personal and generational: a family story carried forward by someone building a public identity of his own.

2. The first tests: discipline, leadership, and recognition

At Massanutten Military Academy in Woodstock, Virginia, Loopy developed discipline and leadership, earned the cadet rank of Second Lieutenant, and received the Commandant’s Accommodations Medal for actions that helped save two fellow cadets. He later attended Benedict College in Columbia, South Carolina, studying Recreation and Leisure Studies.

His journey also includes recognition through the Prince William County Beat the Odds program: a Phoenix Award, a college scholarship, and a Distinguished Speaker appearance. Those experiences connect his story to young people navigating adversity and looking for a way forward.

3. The work: finding purpose in the land

Farming became more than a subject to talk about. It became practical work: animals, soil, seasons, responsibility, and the daily realities of caring for a farm. Through Da HoodFarmer, Loopy brings together experience with livestock, rural life, cowboy culture, and outdoor education.

The brand is grounded in lived experience, not a character created for a camera. The camera may capture the work, but the work exists when nobody is watching.

4. The low point: when the dream gets heavy

In his April 5, 2026 essay, I Almost Quit Everything… Then the Dirt Saved My Life, Loopy describes a period of burnout and doubt. He writes about feeling disconnected from the farm and from the future he had been trying to build. It is an honest account of the part of a journey that rarely appears in a highlight reel: the fatigue behind the ambition.

He does not frame the moment as a neat motivational scene. He describes quiet, a handful of soil, and the realization that the land was not judging him or demanding that he move faster. It was simply alive, patient, and present.

5. The turning point: the dirt teaches patience

That moment changed the way he understood the work. He was not only trying to build a farm; he was rebuilding his relationship with himself and with the future he wanted. The lesson was not to push harder at any cost. It was to respect the time it takes for something real to grow.

That is the heart of the Da HoodFarmer message: dirt don’t lie, and growth cannot be rushed.

6. The return: building something that lasts

Today, Loopy Carter and Da HoodFarmer stand as two connected public identities. Loopy Carter is the public personality and storyteller. Da HoodFarmer is the agricultural, cowboy-lifestyle, and media brand. Together, they create a platform for farming, television, outdoor knowledge, education, partnerships, and new ventures.

The journey is still being written. The goal is not to pretend every chapter has been easy or that every ambition has already been achieved. It is to build a body of work with roots, substance, and a story worth remembering.

“Dirt don’t lie.”

It is more than a slogan. It is the lesson that brought the story back to its foundation.
