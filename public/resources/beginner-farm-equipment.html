Beginner Farm Equipment

Buy for the work you actually have—not the image of a farm.

Equipment decisions can consume a farm budget quickly. The most useful first purchase is not necessarily the biggest machine; it is the tool that safely handles a recurring task, fits the property and can be maintained affordably. Start with a task list before comparing brands or horsepower.

1. List the jobs that must get done

Write down the work required each week and each season: mowing, moving materials, maintaining access paths, feeding animals, cleaning shelters, moving water, repairing fences, handling crops or transporting supplies. Estimate how often each job occurs and how much time it takes with the tools you already have.

2. Separate essential tools from occasional equipment

Basic hand tools, protective equipment, reliable lighting, measuring tools and safe storage may be needed before larger machinery. For specialized jobs that happen only a few times per year, compare rental or contractor costs with the full cost of ownership.

3. Calculate the full cost of ownership

  • Purchase price, financing and taxes.
  • Fuel, fluids, filters and routine service.
  • Repairs, tires, batteries and replacement parts.
  • Storage, transport, insurance and security.
  • Operator training and safety equipment.
  • Downtime and the cost of finding parts or service.

Compare these costs with the cost of renting or hiring the work out. A machine that is inexpensive to buy may still be expensive to keep operating.

4. Match equipment to the property

Consider access width, turning space, slope, soil, ground conditions, storage, implements and the actual loads you need to move. Check the equipment manual and manufacturer specifications. Never assume a machine is suitable because it works on a different farm.

5. Inspect used equipment carefully

Review maintenance records, hours, leaks, tires or tracks, brakes, steering, hydraulics, safety guards, electrical systems and cold-start behavior. Confirm that parts and service are available. For expensive equipment, consider an independent inspection by a qualified mechanic before purchase.

6. Build a maintenance plan

Use the manufacturer's maintenance schedule, keep service records and set aside money for consumables and repairs. A simple log of hours, dates, parts and work performed can prevent missed service and help estimate the true cost per hour.

Plan before you purchase

USDA farm business planning resources recommend identifying the equipment and other resources the operation will need, and evaluating whether the assets you own or rent are adequate. Use the small farm startup budget guide to include equipment and operating costs in the broader plan.

  • USDA National Agricultural Library: Small Farm Funding Guide

This guide is general information, not an equipment specification or safety certification. Follow the operator's manual and obtain qualified training and service where appropriate.
