How to Budget Chicken Feed for a Small Flock

A practical way to estimate recurring feed costs before you buy.

Feed is one of the recurring costs to plan for when keeping chickens. A useful budget starts with the number and type of birds, their actual feed use, the bag size you purchase, and the price you pay locally. A calculator can organize the math, but the inputs need to reflect your flock.

Try the numbers: Use the free chicken feed cost calculator to estimate daily, weekly and 30-day costs with your own flock size and feed price.

1. Start with the right feed estimate

Feed consumption is not identical for every bird. Age, body size, breed, production level, weather, forage access and feed management all affect intake. As a planning reference, Alabama Cooperative Extension describes an average laying hen as consuming roughly 0.25 pound of feed per day, while other extension guidance gives a range that can vary with bird size and conditions. Treat that figure as a starting point for an adult layer—not a universal ration for chicks, pullets or meat birds.

Use the feed manufacturer's directions and reputable poultry guidance for the bird's age and purpose. A complete, age-appropriate ration is important; casually diluting it with scratch grain or homemade mixtures can change the nutrient balance.

2. Calculate the quantity you need

For a simple estimate, multiply the number of birds by the average pounds of feed per bird per day, then multiply by the number of days in your planning period.

  • Daily feed: flock size × pounds per bird per day.
  • Period feed: daily feed × number of days.
  • Bags needed: period feed ÷ pounds per bag.
  • Estimated cost: bags needed × price per bag.

For illustration only, ten adult laying hens at 0.25 pound each would use about 2.5 pounds per day. A 50-pound bag would last about 20 days before allowing for spillage or changes in consumption. Your measured use should replace this example when you have it.

3. Add the costs that a feed-only estimate misses

A feed calculator does not include bedding, feeders, waterers, storage containers, delivery fees, taxes, waste, repairs or veterinary care. Keep these as separate budget lines so the feed figure does not get mistaken for the total cost of keeping a flock.

4. Store feed carefully and track what disappears

Keep feed dry, protected from pests and contamination, and follow the manufacturer's storage guidance and best-before information. Record the date a bag is opened, its weight and cost, and roughly when it is empty. That gives you a better estimate than relying on a generic online average.

5. Keep clean water available

Feed planning should be considered alongside reliable access to clean water. Water needs change with weather and bird conditions. Check drinkers regularly and consult poultry-extension guidance if you are unsure how to size your system.
