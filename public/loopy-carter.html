DMV roots and the Carter family story

Loopy Carter’s story begins with the people, places, and traditions of the Washington, D.C.–Maryland–Northern Virginia region. He was raised within the Carter family and its traditions, a family whose history is deeply connected to Vienna, Freedom Hill, and the land that later became part of the Tysons area in Northern Virginia.

His grandfather is Grailing J. Carter, and his aunts include De Armond “Dee Dee” Carter and Gloria Runyon. A 2014 City of Manassas Black History Month proclamation recognized Grailing J. Carter as a U.S. Air Force veteran and for his work helping veterans with disability claims through the American Legion and Disabled American Veterans. His aunts’ connection to this history is living and active: family members have worked to preserve, document, and share the Carter legacy. That heritage is not simply a surname; it is a family connection to land, farming, community memory, and the history of Black and Indigenous families in Northern Virginia.

Public historical materials document the story of Keziah Hatton Carter, a free Black woman with Tauxenent and Pamunkey ancestry, who purchased 50 acres in 1842 from land associated with the former Wolf Trap plantation. The land became known as Cartersville and Freedom Hill. Her family lived and farmed there across generations. Local-history accounts also record that Keziah purchased the freedom of her husband, Robert, and that they raised ten children. Her son Robert Carter Jr. acquired an additional ten acres in 1859 that later became part of the Town of Vienna.

The family’s connection to that history continues in public life. Later generations included Charles Henry Carter and Carrie Neal-Carter, whose family farm became a lasting part of Vienna’s history. Carrie was remembered as a midwife, and local historical records identify Charles Henry’s sister Lucy Carter as a Civil War-era Union spy. Family accounts recorded by the Fairfax County Times also describe the farm enduring wartime hardship, including soldiers taking livestock. Carter descendants have participated in preservation work, historical interpretation, and efforts to recognize the family’s contribution to Vienna. Fairfax County has taken up the long-term preservation of the Carter Family Cemetery, where descendants have sought to protect the burial ground and its history. In 2021, family members took part in the unveiling of historical markers at Freedom Hill Park. In 2022, the Town of Vienna renamed Wade Hampton Drive as Liberty Lane after a community effort initiated by Dee Dee Carter; Gloria Runyon also served on the town’s ad hoc committee. The Town’s 2026 draft Comprehensive Plan also recognizes the Carter family through the planned Vienna-Carter Library name. The library’s naming recognizes the family’s place in local history, including William McKinley Carter’s civic work and advocacy for an integrated public library.

Family oral history also preserves stories about the family’s Powhatan connections, including a grandmother remembered in family accounts as Kazaya/Kazia Powhatan Carter, and the 1752 burning of the first Fairfax County courthouse. These traditions are part of the family’s own account of its ancestry and relationship to the land; they are presented as oral history, distinct from details independently established in government records.

Education and leadership

Loopy Carter attended Massanutten Military Academy in Woodstock, Virginia, where he developed a foundation in discipline, accountability, and leadership. He earned the cadet rank of Second Lieutenant and received the Commandant’s Accommodations Medal for actions that helped save two fellow cadets. This was military-academy leadership experience, not a claim of armed-forces service.

He later attended Benedict College in Columbia, South Carolina, studying Recreation and Leisure Studies. His education connected leadership and recreation with outdoor programming, community engagement, and the practical skills that would continue to shape his work.

Historical research on the wider Carter family also records Lucy Carter, sister of Charles Henry Carter, as a Civil War-era Union spy. The Town of Vienna’s historical committee report preserves this part of the family story alongside the family’s longstanding role in the community.

Beat the Odds recognition

His personal journey has also been recognized through the Prince William County Beat the Odds program. His recognitions include a Phoenix Award, a college scholarship, and a Distinguished Speaker appearance. The program’s focus on young people overcoming adversity is an important part of the story he carries forward.

Agriculture, livestock, and the outdoors

As a Certified Master Farmer, Loopy Carter brings hands-on agricultural experience to the Da HoodFarmer identity. His experience includes work across an 88-acre farm and time caring for and working with rabbits, chickens, ducks, pigs, horses, cattle, geese, quail, and peafowl. The 88-acre figure describes farm experience; it is not a claim of current ownership.

He is also a Senior Wilderness Survival Instructor, sharing practical outdoor knowledge and survival skills through in-person and remote instruction. Farming, livestock, land stewardship, outdoor readiness, and practical problem-solving are connected parts of his public work.

Television and media

Loopy Carter appeared on Lifetime’s Dr. Pimple Popper, in the “Holy Moly” segment (Season 1, Episode 7). The appearance is part of his television and entertainment record and connects the public personality to the Da HoodFarmer brand.

His media profile is suited to television and digital appearances, interviews, documentary and reality programming, hosting, brand partnerships, agricultural programming, and outdoor-lifestyle content.

Entrepreneurship and the next chapter

Loopy Carter approaches entrepreneurship as an extension of practical experience: identify a need, build something useful, and create value that can grow. His developing projects span agriculture, media, education, digital products, and commercial opportunities. Projects still in development are presented as such—not as established operating companies.
